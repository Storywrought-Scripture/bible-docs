Translation notes

Genesis 10

The Table of Nations

10:1, 32

ʾēlleh tôlᵉḏōṯ bᵉnê-nōaḥ (“These are the generations of the sons of Noah”): the structural formula again, opening and closing the chapter so that the whole table sits inside one frame. What sits inside it is unlike anything before it in Genesis. This is not a family tree. It is a map — seventy peoples arranged by where they live, what they speak and whom they trade with, written in the vocabulary of descent because that is the vocabulary this culture had for political relationship. Read it as a list of ancestors and most of it is unintelligible; read it as the known world seen from the eastern Mediterranean and it comes into focus at once. The order is the first thing to notice: Japheth, then Ham, then Shem. That is the reverse of the order the sons are always named in (5:32; 6:10; 9:18; and verse 1 of this chapter), and it is the narrowing funnel Genesis uses everywhere — the lines that will not be followed are dealt with first and dismissed, and the line the book is actually about is left till last and carried forward into chapter 11. Cain before Seth, Ishmael before Isaac, Esau before Jacob; here, the whole world before Shem.

10:5, 20, 31

lᵉmišpᵉḥōṯām lilšōnōṯām bᵉʾarṣōṯām bᵉḡôyēhem (“by their families and their languages, in their lands and their nations”): the refrain that closes each of the three sections, and it names the four things the table is actually sorting by — kinship, speech, territory, political identity. mišpāḥâ is held to “families”, as at 8:19, where the animals leave the ark lᵉmišpᵉḥōṯêhem; the same word covers a household, a clan and a people, and using it of the animals and then of the nations is the narrator's own link rather than mine. The hard problem is lilšōnōṯām, “their languages.” The nations here are already speaking different tongues — and chapter 11 opens with “the whole earth had one language and the same words,” and then explains how that ended. The two chapters cannot both be describing the same moment. The usual account, and I think the right one, is that the arrangement is thematic and not chronological: chapter 10 shows the outcome and chapter 11 goes back to explain it, which is a normal move in Hebrew narrative (compare 2:4–25 retelling chapter 1). Source-critical readers instead take the two as separate traditions set side by side without harmonising. Either way the tension is in the text and not produced by translating it, and I have not smoothed it.

10:2

bᵉnê yep̄eṯ (“the sons of Japheth”): the northern and western arc, from Anatolia round to the Aegean. māḏay is Media, the Medes of the Iranian plateau. yāwān is Ionia, and it is the standing Hebrew word for Greece thereafter. tuḇal and mešeḵ are Tabal and Mushki, kingdoms in eastern Anatolia known from Assyrian records, and they appear with māḡôḡ again in Ezekiel 38–39 — a passage that has carried a great deal of later prophetic freight, none of which is in view here, where they are simply peoples on the northern rim. gōmer is generally identified with the Cimmerians. Nothing in this list is being praised or condemned; it is an inventory.

10:4

dōḏānîm (“the Dodanim”): a textual variant of the plainest kind. The parallel list at 1 Chronicles 1:7 reads rōḏānîm, and the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint agree with Chronicles against the Masoretic Text here. In the Hebrew square script ḏ and r differ by a stroke and are confused constantly, so one of the two is a copying slip and there is no way to tell from the letters alone which. Rodanim would be Rhodes, which fits a verse otherwise made of Mediterranean sea peoples — kittîm is Kition on Cyprus, taršîš a distant western port associated with metal, ʾĕlîšâ probably Alashiya, also Cyprus. That coherence is an argument for Rodanim and it is not a decisive one, since a copyist could have produced the tidier reading. I translated the Masoretic Dodanim and record the alternative.

10:6

kûš ûmiṣrayim ûp̄ûṭ ûḵᵉnāʿan: the southern arc — Nubia, Egypt, Put — generally identified with Libya — and then Canaan, which is the entry that gives the whole table away. miṣrayim is a dual form, presumably Upper and Lower Egypt, and it is the ordinary Hebrew name for the country. Canaan does not belong here on any linguistic reckoning: Canaanite is a Semitic language, close enough to Hebrew that Israelite and Canaanite speech were mutually intelligible, and a table sorted by descent from a common tongue would have put Canaan with Shem. It is placed under Ham because in the world this list describes Canaan lay inside Egypt's sphere of control, and because of 9:25. The table sorts by political and geographic reality, not by language or by blood, and this verse is the proof of it. The same point returns at verse 22 from the other side, where Elam is filed under Shem though Elamite is not a Semitic language at all.

10:8–9

nimrōḏ … gibbōr-ṣayiḏ lip̄nê YHWH: the one figure in the chapter who gets a story instead of an entry, and the story is four lines long and almost entirely ambiguous. hûʾ hēḥēl lihyôṯ gibbōr bāʾāreṣ, “he was the first to become a mighty one,” uses hēḥēl — the same verb as 9:20 — though here with a complementary infinitive that 9:20 conspicuously lacks —'s “Noah made a beginning.” Both men begin something the narrator declines to evaluate. gibbôr is the warrior-hero, the word behind “the mighty men of old, the men of renown” at 6:4, which is not an encouraging place for it to have been used last. The crux is lip̄nê YHWH, “before the Lord.” It can mean in the Lord's estimation — a hunter of genuinely extraordinary standing, the phrase functioning as a superlative, which is how the proverb quoted in the verse most naturally works. Or it can mean in the Lord's face, in defiance, which is how a long rabbinic tradition took it, reading Nimrod as the first tyrant and the builder of Babel's tower. The Hebrew supports both and the narrator says nothing to settle it. Worth knowing for either reading: the royal hunt was Assyrian state propaganda, carved across palace walls, the king killing lions to display his fitness to rule — so “mighty hunter” in a verse that runs straight on into a list of Mesopotamian capitals is a description of kingship, not of a hobby. No Nimrod has been identified with confidence in any Mesopotamian record.

10:10–12

bāḇel wᵉʾereḵ wᵉʾakkaḏ … bᵉʾereṣ šinʿār: the roll-call of southern Mesopotamia — Babylon, Uruk, Akkad, in Sumer. šinʿār is Shinar, the same land where the tower will be built in 11:2, so the chapter has quietly named Babel twice before chapter 11 begins. Verse 11 has a real grammatical ambiguity: min-hāʾāreṣ hahîʾ yāṣāʾ ʾaššûr can be read “from that land he went out to Assyria,” continuing Nimrod northward, or “from that land Asshur went out,” making Asshur the subject and the founder of the Assyrian cities. Both are good Hebrew. I followed the first, because the paragraph is about Nimrod throughout and a new subject would normally be marked, and because it yields the sharper picture — one empire reaching from Babylon to Nineveh, which is what Mesopotamian power actually looked like. The second reading has the advantage that Asshur is a real name in this chapter, listed under Shem at verse 22. hîʾ hāʿîr haggᵉḏōlâ, “that is the great city,” has an unclear antecedent — Resen, or Nineveh, or the whole four-city complex taken together. Nineveh is the one the rest of the Bible calls great (Jonah 1:2; 3:2–3), which is the argument for it, and the word order is against it.

10:13–14

lûḏîm … kap̄tōrîm: every name in these two verses is a plural, and they are peoples rather than persons — the Ludim, the Anamim, and so on — which is why they are left in their Hebrew form rather than turned into individuals. paṯrusîm is Pathros, Upper Egypt, and is recognisable; the rest are not securely identified. The parenthesis is the problem. “The Casluhim — from whom the Philistines came — and the Caphtorim” puts the Philistines' origin with the Casluhim, but everywhere else in the Bible the Philistines come from Caphtor: Amos 9:7 and Jeremiah 47:4 both say so plainly, and Caphtor is generally identified with Crete, which matches what is known archaeologically of Philistine origins in the Aegean. The likeliest explanation is that the clause has slipped one name to the left in copying and belongs after Caphtorim. I have left it where the Hebrew puts it rather than relocating it, and recorded the difficulty; moving a clause to make a verse agree with Amos is emendation, not translation.

10:15–19

ûḵᵉnaʿan yālaḏ: the longest single entry in the table, and it is not neutral. Canaan's “sons” are the peoples Israel will later be told to dispossess — Heth (the Hittites), the Jebusites of Jerusalem, the Amorites, the Girgashites, the Hivites — followed by a string of coastal cities — Arka, Sin, Arvad, Zemar — and Hamath inland on the Orontes, with Sidon named first as the firstborn. Then verse 19 does something no other entry in the chapter does: it draws a border. From Sidon down to Gaza, and inland to Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah and Zeboiim. That is the map of the land of promise, described a chapter before Abram is born and three chapters before he is told to walk its length, and the cities of the plain are named here nine chapters before they are destroyed. A genealogy that stops to survey a frontier is doing political work, and this one knows exactly what land it is talking about.

10:21

ʾăḥî yep̄eṯ haggāḏôl (“the older brother of Japheth”): genuinely ambiguous, and the ambiguity is about Shem. The Hebrew can mean “the elder brother of Japheth” (Shem is older) or “the brother of Japheth the elder” (Japheth is older). Word order slightly favours attaching haggāḏôl to Japheth, the nearer noun; the consistent listing of Shem first everywhere else favours Shem. Nothing decides it, and the ancient versions split. I chose “the older brother of Japheth,” which is the majority English reading. ʾăḇî kol-bᵉnê-ʿēḇer (“the father of all the sons of Eber”) is placed here, before the list even starts, because ʿēḇer is the point of the whole Shem section — the name behind ʿiḇrî, “Hebrew.” Caution is owed: the derivation is likely but not certain, and ʿiḇrî may instead relate to ʿāḇar, “to cross over,” or to the ʿapiru of second-millennium texts, a social category rather than an ethnic one. The narrator's interest in Eber is not in doubt even if the etymology is.

10:22

ʿêlām wᵉʾaššûr wᵉʾarpaḵšaḏ wᵉlûḏ waʾărām: five entries, and two of them cut against any attempt to read this table as a language family. Elam, east of Mesopotamia, spoke Elamite, which is not Semitic and is not related to Hebrew in any way. Lud has already appeared under Ham at verse 13 in plural form. Asshur is filed under Shem here and is the subject-or-object of verse 11 over in the Hamite section. These are not errors to be reconciled. They are what happens when a list sorts by proximity, alliance and trade rather than by descent — see the note at verse 6. Aram is the Aramaeans of Syria, whose language will eventually displace Hebrew as the common speech of the region.

10:24

ʾarpaḵšaḏ yālaḏ ʾeṯ-šālaḥ: the Septuagint inserts a generation here that the Masoretic Text does not have — Arpachshad fathers Kenan, and Kenan fathers Shelah. The same extra name appears in the Septuagint at 11:12–13 and in Luke's genealogy of Jesus at Luke 3:36, which follows the Greek. So a reader comparing Luke with a Hebrew-based Old Testament will find one more generation in the New Testament than in the Old, and this verse is where it enters. I translated the Masoretic text, which has no Kenan. Whether the Greek preserves a name the Hebrew lost or added one to regularise the list is not settled, and the chronological figures attached to it in Genesis 11 differ systematically between the two traditions in ways that suggest deliberate schematising somewhere.

10:25

peleḡ kî ḇᵉyāmāyw nip̄lᵉḡâ hāʾāreṣ (“Peleg, because in his days the earth was divided”): a name given for a verb, as with Cain at 4:1 — peleḡ and nip̄lᵉḡâ are the same root, pālaḡ, to split or divide. Two readings, and the choice matters more than it looks. If hāʾāreṣ means the earth, and the dividing is the scattering of peoples, this is a direct cross-reference to Babel in 11:1–9 — the chapter planting the explanation it has not given yet. If hāʾāreṣ means the land and pālaḡ is taken in its concrete sense, it refers to irrigation: a peleḡ elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible is a water channel (Psalm 1:3; Isaiah 30:25), and the verse would record the cutting of canals, which is exactly what transformed southern Mesopotamia in this period. The first reading is the traditional one and fits the chapter's own concerns; the second fits the noun's ordinary meaning better. I rendered it “the earth was divided,” which keeps both open in English as the Hebrew does, and did not decide it in the wording.

10:26–30

yoqṭān: thirteen sons, and the addresses are Arabian. ḥăṣarmāweṯ is Hadramaut in southern Arabia and is still on the map; šᵉḇāʾ is Sheba, whose queen visits Solomon; ʾôp̄îr is the gold source of 1 Kings 9:28 and 10:11, never securely located; ḥăwîlâ was already named at 2:11 as the land the river Pishon encircles, where there is gold. Verse 30 gives their range from Mesha to Sephar, “the hill country of the east,” which is as near as the chapter comes to a second border description. The line matters structurally rather than narratively: Joktan is the branch of Shem that the book will not follow. His brother Peleg is, and 11:16 picks that thread up and runs it to Abram. Thirteen sons are listed here and then dropped, which is the funnel doing its work one last time.
