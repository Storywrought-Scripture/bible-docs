Translation notes

Genesis 38

Judah and Tamar

38:1

wayᵉhî bāʿēṯ hahîʾ (“And it happened at that time”): the chapter interrupts the Joseph narrative between the sale and Potiphar's house, and its placement has often been called intrusive. It is not. The two chapters are tied together by a deliberate pattern: in 37 Judah proposes the sale of a brother and the brothers deceive their father with a goat and a garment and the words hakker-nāʾ, “look it over, please” (37:32); here Judah is deceived by way of a promised goat and a garment, and is confronted with the same two words at verse 25. And Judah, who told his father to identify a dead son's robe, is told to identify his own pledge. Note also the movement: wayyēreḏ yᵉhûḏâ, Judah goes down from his brothers, just as Joseph is being taken down to Egypt. The narrator makes the argument by vocabulary and never states it.

38:8

yabbēm ʾōṯāh (“do the duty of a brother-in-law for her”): levirate marriage — the obligation of a dead man's brother to father a son by the widow, the son counting as the dead man's for inheritance and name. The institution is legislated at Deuteronomy 25:5–10, which also provides the ceremony of release for a brother who refuses, and it stands behind the book of Ruth. Here it is assumed as customary without any law being cited, and the household's head enforces it. What the chapter is about, structurally, is a woman with no standing except through a son, in a system where three men in a row fail to give her one.

38:9–10

wᵉšiḥēṯ ʾarṣâ (“he would waste it on the ground”): the verse has been badly misused and the point should be stated plainly. The word “onanism” attached the term to the act itself; the text says nothing about the act in general. What the narrator condemns is named precisely in the verse — lᵉḇiltî nᵉṯān-zeraʿ lᵉʾāḥîw, “so as not to give seed to his brother” — and the tense is iterative: he went in to her repeatedly and each time refused the purpose of going in. Onan takes the sexual use of his brother's widow while withholding the son that would give her security and would take a share of the inheritance he otherwise stands to gain as the surviving elder. What is called evil is the exploitation and the fraud, not the biology. Note also that Er's own offence is never specified at all: the text says only that he was evil in the Lord's eyes.

38:11

šᵉḇî ʾalmānâ ḇêṯ-ʾāḇîḵ (“Stay a widow in your father's house”): Judah's stated reason and his real reason are given in the same verse, and they are different. He tells Tamar to wait for Shelah; he says to himself that Shelah may die like the others. He treats her as the cause of two deaths the narrator has attributed directly to God, and sends her back to her father's house — which removes her from his household's obligation without formally releasing her, leaving her unable to marry anyone else. She is left in a permanent legal nowhere, and the chapter's entire action follows from it.

38:14

bᵉp̄eṯaḥ ʿênayim (“at the entrance to Enaim”): a place name, but the words also read as “the opening of the eyes,” and the chapter is built on seeing and failing to see — Judah saw Shua's daughter (verse 2), saw Tamar and took her for a prostitute (verse 15), and finally is told to look over the pledge and does (verses 25–26). Whether Enaim is a real settlement or a name chosen for the pun cannot be settled; Joshua 15:34 has a place called Enam in Judah's territory. I have translated it as the place name and recorded the wordplay.

38:15, 21

zônâ … qᵉḏēšâ (“prostitute” … “consecrated woman”): two different words for what is nominally the same transaction, and the switch is characterisation. The narrator says Judah took her for a zônâ, an ordinary prostitute. When his friend Hirah goes to settle the debt in public, he asks for the qᵉḏēšâ — a “consecrated” woman, the term for a woman attached to a sanctuary — which is the more respectable word, and the men of the place answer using it too. Whether qᵉḏēšâ denotes sacred prostitution in the sense once commonly assumed is now much disputed, and the evidence for institutionalised temple prostitution in the Levant is far weaker than older commentaries assert. What is not in doubt is that Hirah chooses the politer term, and that Judah's concern at verse 23 is being made a laughing-stock rather than being in the wrong.

38:18

ḥōṯāmᵉḵā ûp̄ᵉṯîleḵā ûmaṭṭᵉḵā (“your seal and your cord, and the staff that is in your hand”): Tamar asks for identification. A seal worn on a cord at the neck was a man's legal signature, pressed into clay to authenticate a document, and a staff could be personal in the same way. The Hebrew names only ḥōṯām, “seal,” and does not say what kind. She does not ask for valuables; she asks for the three objects that can prove who he is. It is the shrewdest move in the chapter, and Judah hands them over to a woman he takes for a stranger at the roadside without apparent thought.

38:24, 26

hôṣîʾûhā wᵉṯiśśārēp̄ … ṣāḏᵉqâ mimmennî (“Bring her out, and let her be burned” … “She is more in the right than I”): the double standard is exposed by the narrative rather than denounced by it. Judah has just been to a prostitute himself, in the same chapter, and pronounces a death sentence on his daughter-in-law for the same act — burning being a penalty the law prescribes in only two cases — Leviticus 20:14, a man who takes a woman and her mother, where the man is burned along with them, and Leviticus 21:9, a priest's daughter who prostitutes herself. Neither covers Tamar. Then the evidence arrives and he reverses himself in public. ṣāḏᵉqâ mimmennî is a comparative — “she is more righteous than I,” or “she is in the right rather than I” — and either way it is a verdict he pronounces against himself. On the glossary, ṣaddîq/ṣeḏeq are fixed to “righteous” and ṣᵉḏāqâ to “righteousness” where it is paired with mišpāṭ (18:19, 25); the verb ṣāḏaq is not among the fixed terms. “In the right” is a departure from the word-group, taken because the clause is a verdict rather than an assessment of character — the same reason given for ṣaddîq at Exodus 9:27, and for ṣiḏqāṯî, “my honesty,” at 30:33. This is the first time in Genesis that a man says plainly that he is in the wrong, and says why, and it is Judah, whose change across the Joseph story culminates at 44:33 in his offer to be enslaved in Benjamin's place.

38:27–30

pereṣ … zeraḥ (“Perez” … “Zerah”): twins struggling at birth, and the younger displacing the elder — the pattern of 25:24–26 repeated with the addition that here the reversal happens during the delivery, and the midwife's scarlet thread records an order that does not hold. Perez is from pāraṣ, to break out or breach, and the midwife's exclamation mah-pāraṣtā ʿāleḵā pāreṣ puns on it. Zerah is probably from zāraḥ, to shine or rise, perhaps for the scarlet. The genealogical payoff is large: Ruth 4:18–22 runs the line from Perez to David, and Matthew 1:3 carries it on — so the ancestress of the royal line is a Canaanite widow who obtained her son by disguising herself as a prostitute and entrapping her father-in-law, and the text records it without embarrassment. Ruth 4:12 has the elders of Bethlehem invoke this household as a blessing.
