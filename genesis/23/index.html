Translation notes

Genesis 23

The Death of Sarah, and the Field of Machpelah

23:1

wayyihyû ḥayyê śārâ (“The life of Sarah was…”): Sarah is the only woman in the Hebrew Bible whose lifespan is recorded. The formula is the one used of the patriarchs, and using it of her is a deliberate act by the narrator. The verse then says it twice — a hundred and twenty-seven years, and “these were the years of Sarah's life” — the same doubling the book uses at 11:30 for her barrenness. It is worth noticing what her life has consisted of in the telling: she is taken into two foreign households, laughs at a promise and denies laughing, drives out a slave woman and her son, and is not told what happened on the mountain in the previous chapter. She dies without the narrator recording any word between her and Abraham after chapter 21.

23:2

qiryaṯ ʾarbaʿ hîʾ ḥeḇrôn (“Kiriath-arba, that is Hebron”): the narrator glosses the older name with the current one, as at 14:2–3 and 14:7 — the same habit that produces “Ur of the Chaldeans” at 11:28. lispōḏ … wᵉliḇkōṯāh, “to mourn … and to weep for her,” distinguishes the formal, public lament from private weeping; Hebrew has both words and uses both. wayyāḇōʾ ʾaḇrāhām, “Abraham came in,” has been taken by some readers to imply he was living apart from her — 22:19 leaves him at Beersheba and this verse puts her death at Hebron — though the verb need not carry that weight.

23:4

gēr wᵉṯôšāḇ ʾānōḵî ʿimmāḵem (“I am a stranger and a settler among you”): the exact legal self-description of a man with no title to land. A gēr is a resident outsider under the protection of the community, and a tôšāḇ is a settled non-citizen; the pair together is a formula, and it defines Abraham's standing precisely — he has lived in this country since chapter 12, God has promised him the whole of it at 12:7, 13:15, 15:18 and 17:8, and he cannot bury his wife without asking. The pair returns at Leviticus 25:23, where God tells the whole nation it holds the land on the same terms Abraham holds this field — “for you are foreigners and residents with me” — and in a single worshipper's mouth at Psalm 39:12.

23:6

nᵉśîʾ ʾĕlōhîm (“a prince of God”): idiomatically a superlative — a mighty prince, one of great standing — since Hebrew regularly intensifies with the divine name, as in nap̄tûlê ʾĕlōhîm at 30:8, which the note there reads as “mighty wrestlings,” and in the reading offered for rûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm at 1:2. But the phrase is not only flattery: it is the language of a community acknowledging that this outsider is a person of consequence, offered at the exact moment they are declining to sell him anything. I have kept the traditional “prince of God” — the volume renders nāśîʾ “chief” or “leader” elsewhere (17:20; 25:16; 34:2; Leviticus 4:22; Numbers 1:16), and it is not a royal title — with the idiom noted, because flattening it to “mighty prince” loses the courtesy register that the entire chapter is written in.

23:3–16 (the negotiation) The whole exchange is conducted in elaborate courtesy and every line of it is a bargaining move, which is why a flat English rendering misleads. Abraham asks for a specific cave at the far end of a field, and for it alone. Ephron replies by publicly giving him not just the cave but the whole field — a refusal disguised as generosity, since an outright gift creates an obligation and a field Abraham did not ask for creates a price. Abraham declines the gift and insists on paying. Ephron then names a figure without naming it as a demand: “between me and you, what is that?” Four hundred shekels is a great deal of money. Jeremiah buys a field at Anathoth for seventeen shekels (Jeremiah 32:9), and Omri buys the hill of Samaria — and builds a capital on it — for two talents, six thousand shekels (1 Kings 16:24). Abraham does not haggle, and the narrator does not say whether he was overcharged; he weighs the silver out at once. ʿōḇēr lassōḥēr, “at the rate current among merchants,” specifies a recognised commercial standard of weight, which matters because coinage does not yet exist and silver is weighed.

23:3, 5, 7 (the sons of Heth) bᵉnê-ḥēṯ (“the sons of Heth”): traditionally “the Hittites,” and the identification is not straightforward. The Hittite empire of Anatolia is a great Late Bronze Age power with its own archives, and there is no evidence of an Anatolian Hittite population settled around Hebron in the patriarchal period. Genesis lists Heth as a son of Canaan at 10:15, which places these people among the Canaanite groups rather than among the Anatolians, and the personal names here — Ephron, Zohar — are Semitic, not Hittite. The likeliest reading is that bᵉnê-ḥēṯ denotes a local group whose name coincided with, or was borrowed from, the northern power. I have kept “sons of Heth” where the Hebrew has it and “Hittite” where it uses the gentilic at verse 10, rather than harmonising to one term and hiding the question.

23:17–18

wayyāqom śᵉḏēh ʿep̄rôn (“So the field of Ephron … passed over”): the verse is written as a legal conveyance, and the details are the details a deed carries: the property identified by owner and location, the boundaries stated, and the trees on the land enumerated as part of the transfer. Comparisons have often been drawn with Hittite and Mesopotamian land-transfer documents, which likewise itemise trees, and with the practice of transacting business publicly at the city gate before witnesses — “all who came in at the gate of his city” is the witness clause. Whether the parallels are close enough to date the passage is disputed and this volume does not rely on them; what is not disputed is that the chapter is deliberately written in the register of a property record rather than of a narrative.

23:19–20

laʾăḥuzzaṯ-qāḇer (“as a holding for a grave”): the phrase closes the chapter as it opened the request, and ʾăḥuzzâ is the word for a permanent, inheritable holding — the term used of Israel's allotted land in Leviticus and Joshua. So the first square foot of Canaan that Abraham owns outright, after the promises of the entire country at 12:7, 13:15, 15:18 and 17:8, is a burial plot bought at a steep price from people who called him a stranger. The narrator makes nothing of the irony and does not need to. The cave becomes the family grave: Abraham is buried there (25:9), then Isaac and Rebekah and Leah (49:31), Jacob makes Joseph swear to carry him back to it (47:29–31), charges all his sons with the same as he dies (49:29–32), and is carried there by them (50:13). It is the one fixed point in the land through the whole rest of the book.
