Translation notes

Genesis 26

Isaac in Gerar, and the Wells

26:1

millᵉḇaḏ hārāʿāḇ hāriʾšôn (“besides the first famine”): the narrator points at the earlier story himself. This chapter reruns three episodes from Abraham's life with Isaac in the lead — a famine that drives the family toward Egypt (12:10), a wife passed off as a sister before a king (12:11–20; 20:1–18), and a treaty with Abimelech at Beersheba over a well (21:22–34) — and rather than concealing the repetition the text flags it in its first sentence. Whether these are variants of one tradition attached to different patriarchs, or a deliberate literary pattern showing the son inheriting the father's situations, is a standing question; see the note at 20:1–2. What is distinctive here is that this is the only chapter in Genesis given over to Isaac's own story — until verses 34–35 hand the narrative to Esau. Everywhere else he is the son being bound, the bridegroom being fetched, or the blind old man being deceived.

26:5

mišmartî miṣwōṯay ḥuqqôṯay wᵉṯôrōṯāy (“my charge, my commandments, my statutes and my instructions”): four terms, and they are the standard vocabulary of the Sinai legislation — the language of Deuteronomy, applied to a man who lived centuries before any of it was given. This is widely read as a later hand describing Abraham's obedience in the terms its own readers knew, and it is one of the clearer places in Genesis where the narrator's own period shows through the narrative. Note also the theological weight the verse carries: the promise is renewed to Isaac for Abraham's sake, on the ground of Abraham's obedience, and verse 24 says the same thing again. Isaac receives what he receives because of his father.

26:7

ʾăḥōṯî hîʾ (“She is my sister”): the third of the three episodes, and the differences from the other two are instructive. Here the lie is told to “the men of the place” rather than to a king; nothing happens to Rebekah, who is never taken; God does not intervene at any point; and — unlike Abraham at 20:12 — Isaac has no defence available, since Rebekah is his cousin's daughter and not his sister in any sense. The text also passes no verdict on him whatever, exactly as it passed none on Abraham. Abimelech's rebuke at verse 10, that guilt would have come on the whole people, is the same moral logic as 20:9.

26:8

mᵉṣaḥēq ʾēṯ riḇqâ ʾištô (“laughing with Rebekah his wife”): the same verb, in the same intensive form, as at 21:9, where Sarah sees Ishmael mᵉṣaḥēq and demands his expulsion. Here it plainly carries a sexual or at least unmistakably marital sense, since seeing it is what tells Abimelech they are not siblings; and it is again a play on Isaac's own name, yiṣḥāq. That the word can mean this is exactly why the note at 21:9 refuses to settle what Ishmael was doing. I have kept “laughing” in both places so that an English reader can see the same word doing different work, with the sense recorded here rather than resolved in the text.

26:12

wayyizraʿ yiṣḥāq (“And Isaac sowed”): the only patriarch in Genesis who farms. Abraham and Jacob are herdsmen throughout; Isaac plants a crop and harvests it, and does so as a resident alien on other people's land, which is a legally precarious thing to do. mēʾâ šᵉʿārîm, “a hundredfold,” is an extraordinary yield and the narrator attributes it directly to blessing. Verse 13 then piles the same root up three times — he became great, and went on being great, until he was very great — a stylistic excess that is doing in Hebrew what it does in English.

26:15, 18

sittᵉmûm pᵉlištîm (“the Philistines had stopped up”): filling in a well is an act of deliberate hostility in a semi-arid country and amounts to destroying the means of living on the land. On the anachronism of “Philistines” in the patriarchal narratives, see the note at 21:32. What the chapter adds is the point about names: Isaac re-digs his father's wells and gives them back their father's names, which is a legal claim as much as a filial one — restoring the names is asserting continuous title to the water.

26:20–22

ʿēśeq … śiṭnâ … rᵉḥōḇôṯ (“Esek” … “Sitnah” … “Rehoboth”): three wells named for what happened at them, and the sequence is the point — contention, then hostility, then room. ʿēśeq is from a root for quarrelling or contending; śiṭnâ is from śāṭan, to oppose or accuse, the root that gives the noun śāṭān, the accuser or adversary, though nothing supernatural is in view here; rᵉḥōḇôṯ is “broad places, room,” and Isaac's own explanation puns on it — hirḥîḇ, “has made room.” Note that Isaac's response to being driven off a well twice is simply to move and dig again. It is the only conflict in Genesis resolved by one party repeatedly withdrawing.

26:24

ʾānōḵî ʾĕlōhê ʾaḇrāhām ʾāḇîḵā (“I am the God of Abraham your father”): the first occurrence of the “God of your father” formula that will become standard through the rest of the book (28:13; 31:42; 32:9; 46:3) and beyond it into Exodus 3:6. It identifies God by relationship to a named ancestor rather than by title or place, which is characteristic of the patriarchal narratives and distinguishes their language from the Sinai material. ʾal-tîrāʾ, “do not be afraid,” is the standard opening of such an address and does not imply Isaac had shown fear.

26:33

šiḇʿâ (“Shibah”): the same name-giving as 21:31, where the name Beersheba is given after an oath sworn with an Abimelech and a Phicol over a disputed well. Here Isaac does it again, and the narrator supplies the etymology a second time as though for the first. The two accounts share the place, the name, the dispute, the treaty and two of the three personal names. Either the offices were hereditary and the names dynastic — Abimelech means “my father is king” and may be a title — or these are two tellings of one tradition set side by side. The volume translates both as they stand; see the note at 26:1.

26:34–35

mōraṯ rûaḥ (“a bitterness of spirit”): two verses of genealogy placed exactly where they will do the most narrative work. Esau marries outside the family, and Hittite women at that — the very thing Abraham swore his servant to prevent for Isaac in chapter 24 — and the note that this embittered both parents is the last thing said before Rebekah engineers the deception of chapter 27. Rebekah will reach for this motive again at 27:46, offering Isaac the Hittite wives as her reason for sending Jacob away — true as far as it goes, and not the reason. rûaḥ here is “spirit” in the sense of temper or disposition; on the range of the word see the flag at 8:1.
