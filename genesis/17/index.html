Translation notes

Genesis 17

The Covenant of Circumcision

17:1

ʾănî ʾēl šadday (“I am El Shaddai”): the meaning is genuinely unknown, which is why it is transliterated here rather than translated. “God Almighty” comes to English through the Vulgate's omnipotens, which is not derived from the Hebrew. The ancient translators appear to have been no more certain of the sense than anyone since. Three derivations are commonly proposed: a link to Akkadian šadû, mountain, giving “God of the mountain”; a link to šaḏ, breast, giving a God who nourishes — which would fit a chapter about fertility rather well and is argued on that basis; and a link to šāḏaḏ, to destroy, which is the connection the Hebrew Bible itself hears, punning kᵉšōḏ miššadday at Isaiah 13:6 and Joel 1:15. None is secure. The title clusters in the patriarchal narratives and in Job, and Exodus 6:3 makes a point of saying the patriarchs knew God as ʾēl šadday and not by the name YHWH — a verse this chapter's opening sits oddly beside, since the narrator here says YHWH appeared.

17:1

hiṯhallēḵ lᵉp̄ānay wehyēh ṯāmîm (“Walk before me, and be blameless”): hiṯhallēḵ is the same reflexive-iterative walking used of Enoch at 5:22 and Noah at 6:9 — but with a different preposition, and the difference is worth noticing. Those two walked ʾeṯ hāʾĕlōhîm, with God, alongside him. Abraham is told to walk lᵉp̄ānay, before me, in God's sight — the posture of a servant going ahead of his lord, or of a life lived under observation. It is a demand rather than a description. tāmîm is held to what the note at 6:9 established: not primarily a moral word but the term for a sacrificial animal without blemish — whole, sound, unmaimed. Which is a striking thing to say immediately before commanding a cut in the flesh.

17:5

ʾaḇrāhām kî ʾaḇ-hămôn gôyim (“Abraham, for … the father of a multitude of nations”): the text explains the new name as ʾaḇ, father, plus hămôn, multitude — and philologically that is not what the name means. ʾaḇrāhām is almost certainly a dialectal expansion of ʾaḇrām, “the father is exalted,” with no hămôn in it at all; the two forms are the same name. This is a folk etymology in the same class as Babel at 11:9: not a mistake about word history, but a claim about the person being named, made by sound. It is worth saying plainly rather than defending, because the text's interest is not in linguistics. A name is being changed by the one who gives it, which is an act of ownership; God does this to Abram, to Sarai, and later to Jacob, and in each case the man's future is packed into the syllables regardless of what the syllables originally meant.

17:7–8

bᵉrîṯ ʿôlām … ʾăḥuzzaṯ ʿôlām (“an everlasting covenant” … “an everlasting holding”): hăqimōṯî, “I will establish,” is the hēqîm of 6:18 and 9:9 rather than the kāraṯ, “cut,” of 15:18 — see the note at 6:18 on the distinction and how much weight it will bear. The heart of the passage is the covenant formula in verse 7: lihyôṯ lᵉḵā lēʾlōhîm, “to be God to you,” repeated in verse 8 as “I will be their God.” That clause, and not the land or the offspring, is what the covenant is finally about, and it recurs through the Torah and the prophets as the standing definition of the relationship. Note what has changed since chapter 15: there, God bound himself alone and asked nothing (15:17). Here, for the first time, the covenant itself carries an obligation Abraham must keep (verse 9).

17:10–14

himmôl lāḵem kol-zāḵār (“every male among you is to be circumcised”): circumcision was not an Israelite invention and the Bible knows it. Jeremiah 9:25–26 lists Egypt, Judah, Edom, Ammon, Moab and “all who clip the corners of their hair, who live in the wilderness” — the desert tribes, usually identified as Arab — among the circumcised; a Sixth Dynasty relief in the tomb of Ankhmahor at Saqqara appears to depict the operation; the Philistines are the conspicuous exception and “the uncircumcised” becomes an epithet for them. What is distinctive here is not the practice but its terms. Elsewhere in the region it is done at puberty or before marriage and marks entry into adulthood; here it is done at eight days, on an infant who can neither consent nor understand, and it marks nothing the child has achieved. It is a ʾôṯ, a sign, in the same sense as the bow in the cloud at 9:12–17 — but cut into the body, permanent, private, and carried by the party who did not initiate the covenant. Verse 14's penalty turns on a pun the English cannot hold: the man who is not cut will be cut off, wᵉniḵrᵉṯâ, from his people. What kārēṯ actually entailed — execution, banishment, childlessness, or a divine act rather than a human one — is debated and never spelled out.

17:12–13, 23, 27

yᵉlîḏ bayiṯ ûmiqnaṯ-kesep̄ (“the one born in your household and the one bought with silver”): stated twice in the command, at verses 12 and 13, and twice again in the execution, at verses 23 and 27, which is emphasis. The sign is placed on the whole household, not the bloodline — including foreign slaves who are explicitly “not of your seed” (verse 12), and including Ishmael, who is about to be told he is not the covenant heir and is circumcised anyway on the same day. Whatever the mark means, it does not mean membership by descent, and the text goes out of its way to say so four times.

17:15

śāray … śārâ (“Sarai … Sarah”): unlike Abram's, this change is given no explanation at all — the text simply says the name is now Sarah, and moves on to the blessing. Philologically the two forms mean the same thing: śārâ is “princess,” and śāray is most likely an older or dialectal form of the same word. So a real change is being made with no semantic content whatever, which puts the emphasis entirely on the act. She is being renamed by God, as Abraham was, and the reason is supplied not by an etymology but by what follows: wᵉhāyᵉṯâ lᵉḡôyim, she will become nations. The promise is now hers as well as his, and this is the first time in the book that God speaks of Sarai to Abraham. She has been named once before in divine speech — the messenger addresses Hagar as “slave of Sarai” at 16:8 — but never as a party to the promise.

17:17

wayyippōl ʾaḇrāhām ʿal-pānāyw wayyiṣḥāq (“Abraham fell on his face and laughed”): ṣāḥaq is the root of yiṣḥāq, Isaac — “he laughs” — and the name is given at verse 19, two verses later, without the connection being spelled out. Four people laugh around this child: Abraham here, Sarah at 18:12, the neighbours at 21:6, and Ishmael at 21:9. Abraham is not rebuked and Sarah is. He laughs, falls on his face, and questions the arithmetic aloud in his heart — “can a child be born to a man of a hundred?” — and God's reply simply continues with the promise. Sarah laughs at the same news for the same reason one chapter later and is challenged for it: “Why did Sarah laugh?” The asymmetry is in the text and this volume does not resolve it. It has been explained as a difference between wonder and disbelief, which the Hebrew does not mark, and as a difference in who is doing the reporting, which it does not mark either. Verse 18 is worth reading as part of the same speech: Abraham's counter-proposal is lû yišmāʿēʾl yiḥyeh lᵉp̄āneḵā, let Ishmael live before you — he would rather have the son he has than the one he is being promised.

17:20

ûlᵉyišmāʿēʾl šᵉmaʿtîḵā (“As for Ishmael, I have heard you”): a pun and a promise at once. The boy's name means God hears (16:11), and God's answer to Abraham's plea for him is “I have heard you” — šᵉmaʿtîḵā, the third time the verb itself stands in two chapters — Abram listens to Sarai's voice at 16:2, the Lord hears Hagar's affliction at 16:11, and God now hears Abraham. Ishmael gets the language of blessing in full: fruitful, multiplied exceedingly, a great nation, twelve chiefs (fulfilled at 25:12–16, where the twelve are named). What he does not get is the covenant, and verse 21 states the exclusion as flatly as verse 20 states the blessing. The chapter refuses to soften either half.

17:23–27

bᵉʿeṣem hayyôm hazzeh (“that very day”): God finishes speaking, goes up (verse 22), and Abraham circumcises the entire male population of his household the same day — himself at ninety-nine, Ishmael at thirteen, and every man born in the house or bought with silver. The phrase bᵉʿeṣem hayyôm hazzeh is used twice in five verses, and it is the formula the Torah reserves for acts done exactly and without delay (compare Exodus 12:41, 51). There is no negotiation and no interval. Set against the laughter of verse 17, the chapter's last word on Abraham is that he questioned the promise and obeyed the command on the same afternoon. Islamic tradition notes Ishmael's age here as thirteen, and some Muslim communities have taken it as the model; practice across the Muslim world varies widely, from the first days of life to puberty.
