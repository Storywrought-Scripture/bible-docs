Translation notes

Genesis 3

The Serpent, the Fall, and the Expulsion

3:1

ʿārûm (“cunning, shrewd”): the same sound as the ʿărûmmîm (“naked”) of 2:25 — the narrator ties the couple’s innocence to the serpent’s craft on a pun carried across the chapter break. ʿārûm is not always negative (Proverbs uses it for “prudent”), but here it is cunning. Note the distortion: God permitted every tree but one (2:16–17), and the serpent reframes it as “not from any tree,” baiting an exaggeration — and says “God” (ʾĕlōhîm), dropping the personal name YHWH used everywhere else here.

3:2–3

The woman’s reply quietly alters the command three ways: she drops God’s emphatic “freely eat” (ʾāḵōl tōʾḵēl, 2:16); she adds “and you must not touch it” (not in the original); and she softens the absolute “you will surely die” (môṯ tāmûṯ) to a bare “or you will die” (pen tᵉmuṯûn). The fence around the word is both raised and weakened.

3:4

lōʾ môṯ tᵉmuṯûn: the serpent throws God’s own emphatic construction back, negated — “you will certainly not die” — a flat contradiction of 2:17.

3:5, 22

kēʾlōhîm (“like God / like gods”): ʾĕlōhîm is grammatically plural and can mean “God,” “gods,” or “divine beings” — genuinely ambiguous. At 3:22 God says the man has become “like one of us,” the plural of 1:26 (divine council or deliberation). The serpent’s promise was not simply false: 3:22 confirms a real, if ruinous, knowledge of good and evil was gained.

3:6

“who was with her” (ʿimmāh): the Hebrew places him there when she gives him the fruit — not absent and later told. How long he had been present it does not say; ʿimmāh fixes the moment, not the whole dialogue. Many older translations bury the word; it stands in MT and LXX. The threefold appeal (good for food, delight to the eyes, desirable for wisdom) echoes the trees of 2:9 and adds the lure the serpent supplied.

3:8

lᵉrûaḥ hayyôm (“in the breeze of the day”): rûaḥ is wind / breath / spirit; the traditional “cool of the day” (the evening breeze) is the common reading, and LXX has “in the afternoon.” A minority hears a theophanic “wind / storm of the day.” miṯhallēḵ (hithpael of hālaḵ) — God moves “to and fro” in the garden as if at home in it.

3:13

hiššîʾanî (“deceived, tricked me”): “led me astray.” The woman names deception; the man (3:12) names the woman — and implicates God (“the woman you put here with me”).

3:14, 17

ʾārûr (“cursed”): pronounced here over the serpent and in 3:17 over the ground — but never directly over the man or the woman. Their sentences are spoken, yet the curse-word itself falls on the serpent and the soil. Easily missed, and worth teaching.

3:15

The crux of the chapter. zeraʿ (“seed, offspring”) is a collective singular: it can mean descendants as a group or be read of a single representative, and the grammar sustains both. The pronoun is masculine singular hûʾ, agreeing with zeraʿ. The LXX makes a striking move: though its word for seed (sperma) is neuter, it uses a masculine pronoun autos (“he”). The mismatch allows a reading of one individual rather than a collective, and is where the later messianic reading takes hold; whether the translator intended that is not recoverable. (The Vulgate’s famous ipsa, “she,” referring to the woman, is a variant against the better Latin ipse, “he,” and against the Hebrew.) šûp̄, used identically of both blows, means “strike, crush, snap at” (LXX read it “lie in wait for, watch”). The same verb governs both clauses, so the difference is not in the verb but the target: a blow to the head is mortal, a blow to the heel is not. KJV’s “bruise… bruise” keeps the single verb; “crush… strike” risks loading the asymmetry into the verb. I kept one verb — “strike… strike” — and let “head” and “heel” carry it.

3:16

Two cruxes. (1) harbâ ʾarbeh ʿiṣṣᵉḇônēḵ wᵉhērōnēḵ: ʿiṣṣāḇôn is “toil, pain, hardship” — the very word used of the man’s labour in 3:17, binding the two sentences together; the phrase is likely a hendiadys, “your painful labour in childbearing.” (2) tᵉšûqâ (“desire, longing”) + māšal (“rule”): this exact pair recurs at 4:7, where sin’s tᵉšûqâ is toward Cain and he must māšal over it — a contest for mastery. That parallel drives a reading where the woman’s “desire” is a drive to control and “he will rule over you” names the resulting struggle (so ESV 2016: “your desire shall be contrary to your husband”). The older reading takes tᵉšûqâ as relational longing and “he shall rule” as a plain statement of the new hierarchy. The LXX read the noun differently again — apostrophē, “your recourse / turning shall be to your husband.” I rendered the traditional “your desire will be for your husband”; the 4:7 parallel is the strongest case for the harder “contrary to” reading. Teaching should weigh both. On the glossary: ʿiṣṣāḇôn is fixed to “painful toil” and is rendered “toil” here and at 3:17, the ordinary sense in both places; recorded as a departure.

3:19

ʿāp̄ār (“dust”): “dust you are, and to dust you will return” closes the circle opened at 2:7, where the human was formed from the dust of the ʾăḏāmâ. This is not annihilation-talk but a return to origin — the ground reclaims what was drawn from it.

3:20

Ḥawwâ (“Eve”) ~ ḥay (“living”): a sound-play — she is named Ḥawwâ because she is “mother of all ḥay (the living).” Note the timing: the man names her life-giver immediately after the death sentence is pronounced — an act that reads as hope, or defiance, against the verdict just spoken. (Also: hā-ʾāḏām, rendered “the human” in ch. 2, I render “the man” through this narrative; it edges into the proper name “Adam” from 3:17 on.)

3:21

koṯnôṯ ʿôr (“garments of skin”): God’s first act after the sentence is to clothe them — and skin garments imply the death of an animal, the first such death in the narrative — though the text never describes a killing, or says where the skins came from — God covering what their fig leaves (3:7) could not.

3:22–24

gāraš (“drove out,” 3:24) is forceful — expelled, banished — stronger than the gentler šillaḥ (“sent out,” 3:23); the narrator uses both, escalating. kᵉrûḇîm (cherubim) are guardian figures (cf. the ark, the temple); the lahaṭ haḥereḇ hammiṯhappeḵeṯ (“the flame of the whirling sword,” a sword-flame turning every way) bars return. Eden is not destroyed but closed, its tree of life now guarded.
