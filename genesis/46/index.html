Translation notes

Genesis 46

Israel Goes Down to Egypt

46:1

bᵉʾērâ šāḇaʿ (“Beersheba”): the last stop before leaving the land, and the place is chosen. It is where Abraham planted a tamarisk and called on the Everlasting God (21:33), where Isaac was told do not be afraid, for I am with you (26:24), and where Jacob himself set out from when he fled to Haran (28:10). He is leaving Canaan for the second time and stops at the same border to ask. He sacrifices lēʾlōhê ʾāḇîw yiṣḥāq, to the God of his father Isaac — not of Abraham, which is unusual; the address to Isaac may be because it was to Isaac that the prohibition on going down to Egypt was given at 26:2, and that is precisely the permission Jacob now needs.

46:3–4

ʾal-tîrāʾ mērᵉḏâ miṣrayᵉmâ (“Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt”): the fear is well founded and the reassurance is exact. Abram went down to Egypt in a famine and it went badly (12:10–20); Isaac was expressly forbidden to (26:2); and God has promised this family a different land on seven separate occasions — 12:7, 13:15, 15:7–18, 17:8, 26:3–4, 28:13 and 35:12. This is the last time God speaks to anyone in Genesis. The promise has four parts, and they set up the whole of Exodus: a great nation there, God going down with them, God bringing them up again — ʾaʿalᵉḵā ḡam-ʿālōh, the emphatic that becomes the verb of the exodus — and, added last and out of scale with the rest, wᵉyôsēp̄ yāšîṯ yāḏô ʿal-ʿênêḵā, “and Joseph will lay his hand on your eyes,” the gesture of closing a dead man's eyes. A nation is promised and a son is promised at the deathbed, and the old man is presumably listening hardest to the second.

46:8–27

The list is a formal census of the household, grouped by the four mothers, and it is not without difficulties. Er and Onan are listed among those who came to Egypt and then noted as having died in Canaan (verse 12); Perez's sons Hezron and Hamul are included though Perez was born only a few years earlier in the sequence of chapter 38; and Benjamin, the boy of chapters 43–44, is credited with ten sons. The list is best read as a record of the tribal house as it stood later, cast as a travelling roster — a standard feature of such genealogies rather than a set of errors to be reconciled. Note that Dinah is named (verse 15) and Serah daughter of Asher (verse 17), two of very few women in the roll; and that Shaul is marked as the son of a Canaanite woman, the kind of detail the lists usually pass over.

46:26–27

kol-hannep̄eš … šiḇʿîm (“all the persons … seventy”): the arithmetic is stated twice and the two counts are reached differently — sixty-six who came out of Jacob's own body, then seventy for the household including Joseph and his two sons and, on the usual reckoning, Jacob himself. The Septuagint reads seventy-five, adding further descendants of Joseph, and that is the figure Stephen quotes at Acts 7:14; a Qumran fragment of Exodus carries the higher figure at the parallel count. Seventy is a round and symbolic total in this literature — seventy nations in chapter 10, seventy elders at Sinai — and the point of the verse is not the tally but the smallness of it: the nation promised at verse 3 leaves Canaan as seventy people in borrowed wagons. Exodus 1:5 repeats the number, and Exodus 1:7 answers it.

46:29

wayyēḇk ʿal-ṣawwāʾrāyw ʿôḏ (“and wept on his neck a long time”): the fifth of Joseph's seven weepings — the list is at 42:24 — and the syntax is worth keeping. The Hebrew has Joseph doing all the verbs — harnessing, going up, appearing, falling on his neck, weeping — and Jacob does nothing at all until he speaks in verse 30. The old man is simply wept over. Note wayyērāʾ ʾēlāyw, “and he appeared to him,” a verb used in this book almost exclusively of God appearing to the patriarchs; using it of a son arriving in a chariot is either a deliberate echo or a plain sense of coming into view, and it is impossible to decide.

46:30

ʾāmûṯâ happāʿam (“Now let me die”): Jacob does not, in fact, die for another seventeen years (47:28), and the line is not a prediction but a release — the same sentiment as Simeon's at Luke 2:29 in a much later text. It answers his own words at 37:35, I will go down to my son, mourning, to Sheol, and at 42:38, 44:29 and 44:31, where he three times foresaw his grey head going down to Sheol — in sorrow at 42:38 and 44:31, in misery at 44:29, the Hebrew changing the noun in the middle of the three. The mourning that has framed his speech since chapter 37 ends in this sentence.

46:31–34

kî-ṯôʿăḇaṯ miṣrayim kol-rōʿēh ṣōʾn (“because every shepherd is detestable to the Egyptians”): Joseph coaches his family in what to say, and the coaching is a piece of political management. He will tell Pharaoh they are shepherds; he instructs them to say they are men of livestock — ʾanšê miqneh, a broader and more respectable term covering herd-owners — and the aim of the whole manoeuvre is stated: so that you may settle in the land of Goshen, apart, on the frontier, rather than being absorbed into Egypt. Whether the Egyptian contempt for shepherds reflects a real attitude — settled agriculturalists' disdain for pastoralists, or a memory of Semitic rule in the Delta — is much discussed and not settled; the same word tôʿēḇâ was used of eating with Hebrews at 43:32. What matters narratively is that Joseph secures his family a separate territory by trading on Egyptian prejudice, and that, read against the opening of Exodus, the separateness is what is still in place when the story resumes.
