Translation notes

Genesis 16

Hagar, and the God Who Sees

16:2–3

wayyišmaʿ ʾaḇrām lᵉqôl śāray … wattiqqaḥ … wattittēn (“Abram listened to the voice of Sarai” … “took” … “and gave”): the chapter opens by quoting chapter 3. The man who listened to the voice of his wife is the charge God brings against the first man at 3:17, in those words; and Sarai took and gave to her husband, which is 3:6 exactly — wattiqqaḥ … wattittēn gam-lᵉʾîšāh. Three verbal echoes in two verses, and the narrator makes no comment on any of them. He does not need to: a reader who has come this far knows what that sequence produced last time. ʾibbāneh mimmennâ, “perhaps I will be built up through her,” puns on bānâ, to build, and bēn, son — to be built is to be given a son, and English cannot hold both.

16:1–3 (the arrangement) What Sarai proposes is not improvised. Giving a slave to one's husband to bear an heir on the wife's behalf is a recognised legal arrangement across the ancient Near East — it appears in the Code of Hammurabi and in marriage contracts from Nuzi, some of which require a barren wife to provide a substitute, and stipulate what happens to the resulting child. The child would be reckoned the wife's. So Sarai is doing the ordinary, legally sound thing, and the narrator reports it without either endorsing or condemning the institution. Note the words for Hagar's status: she is šip̄ḥâ, a female slave, in verses 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 8, and she is given as a wife in verse 3 — both at once, and the collision of those two positions is the engine of everything that follows.

16:4–5

wattēqal gᵉḇirtāh bᵉʿênêhā (“her mistress became small in her eyes”): qālal is to be light, of little weight — the same verb as 12:3, where God promises to curse the one who treats Abram lightly. Hagar's pregnancy inverts the household's whole order of weight, and Sarai uses the same phrase back about herself in verse 5. Sarai's opening words are stronger than they look: ḥămāsî ʿāleḵā, “the violence done to me is on you” — ḥāmās is the word for predatory wrong that filled the earth in 6:11 and brought the flood. She is not saying she is upset. She is charging Abram with an outrage and demanding that God adjudicate: yišpōṭ YHWH bênî ûḇêneḵā.

16:6

wattᵉʿannehā śāray wattiḇraḥ mippānêhā (“Then Sarai afflicted her, and she ran away from her”): the verb is ʿānâ, and it is the single most loaded word in the chapter. It is what the Egyptians will do to Israel — lᵉmaʿan ʿannōṯô bᵉsiḇlōṯām at Exodus 1:11, which this volume renders “to wear them down with forced labour,” with the root again at 1:12 — and it is named in God's own summary of the slavery at 15:13, one chapter ago, in the sentence wᵉʿinnû ʾōṯām. Here a Hebrew woman does it to an Egyptian slave, and the Egyptian flees into the wilderness, which is the other half of the Exodus vocabulary. Genesis puts the Exodus in miniature and reverses who is who. Note also Abram's contribution to the scene, which is one sentence long and consists of surrendering her: hinnēh šip̄ḥāṯēḵ bᵉyāḏēḵ, she is in your hand, do what is good in your eyes.

16:7

malʾaḵ YHWH (“the angel of the Lord”): the first occurrence in the Bible, and it arrives to an Egyptian slave girl rather than to Abram. malʾāḵ means messenger, human or divine; the word carries no wings, no radiance and no description, and per this volume's rules such a figure is not depicted beyond what scripture states of it. The identity problem starts here and is never resolved: the messenger speaks in the first person as God — “I will greatly multiply your seed” (verse 10) — and in verse 13 Hagar names YHWH who had spoken to her, not a messenger. The same slippage recurs at 22:11–18, Exodus 3:2–6 and Judges 6. Whether the messenger is a distinct being speaking for God, a mode of God's own presence, or a way of narrating an encounter that cannot be narrated directly, the Hebrew does not say, and translating it either way would settle it.

16:9

šûḇî ʾel-gᵉḇirtēḵ wᵉhiṯʿannî taḥaṯ yāḏêhā (“Go back to your mistress and submit to her hand”): the hardest instruction in the chapter and it is not softened here. The verb is ʿānâ again, now reflexive — she is told to submit herself to the affliction she has just escaped, and to go back under the hand of the woman who inflicted it. The text offers no justification and no comfort in this verse. What it offers instead is the next three verses: the promise that follows is the seed-multiplication promise itself, and it is given to her before she goes. That is the shape of the passage and it is left as it stands. Reading verse 9 as approval of Sarai's treatment is not available — verse 11 has God calling it ʿŏnyēḵ, your affliction, using the same root a third time.

16:10–11

harbâ ʾarbeh ʾeṯ-zarʿēḵ (“I will greatly multiply your seed”): the promise of 13:16, 15:5 and 22:17 spoken to a woman, and she is the only woman in the Hebrew Bible to whom God speaks it directly — Rebekah receives the same formula at 24:60, but from her own family. zeraʿ is held to the glossary's “seed,” which is what lets an English reader see that it is the same promise. The son's name is then explained: yišmāʿēʾl is yišmaʿ plus ʾēl, God hears — “because the Lord has heard your affliction.” The naming is the more striking because nobody in the chapter has spoken to God at all. Hagar has not prayed; she has run. What God has heard is the affliction itself.

16:12

pereʾ ʾāḏām (“a wild donkey of a man”): not the insult it sounds like in English. The pereʾ is the onager, the wild ass of the desert, and Job 39:5–8 makes it the emblem of a creature loosed from every owner — scorning the tumult of the city, deaf to the driver's shouts, at home in the salt wastes. It is a portrait of untamed independence, and for a people at the desert margins it is closer to a description than a slur. The rest of the verse is harder and less generous: yāḏô ḇakkōl wᵉyaḏ kōl bô, his hand against everyone and everyone's hand against him, a life of perpetual conflict. The final clause, ʿal-pᵉnê ḵol-ʾeḥāyw yiškōn, can mean he will dwell in defiance of all his kin, alongside them, or to the east of them; the same phrase is used of Ishmael's descendants at 25:18, where a geographical sense fits well. I rendered it “in the face of,” which keeps the confrontation and the location both live, as the Hebrew does.

16:13

ʾattâ ʾēl rŏʾî (“You are a God who sees me”): Hagar is the only person in the Hebrew Bible who gives God a name, and she is an Egyptian slave woman with no standing in the household she has fled. ʾēl rŏʾî is “God of my seeing” or “God who sees me,” the ambiguity being whether the seeing is hers or his. The clause that follows is one of the most corrupt-looking sentences in Genesis — hăḡam hălōm rāʾîṯî ʾaḥărê rōʾî — and no rendering of it is confident. Word for word it runs something like “have I even here seen after the one who sees me,” which may mean “have I really seen God and lived to look back?”, or “have I gone on seeing after he saw me?”, or something the consonants no longer carry. The versions differ and several modern translations emend. I have translated it close to the letter and left it strange, because smoothing it would hide that the text is damaged or idiomatic beyond recovery here. The well's name in verse 14 keeps the root and replaces the other: bᵉʾēr laḥay rōʾî, the well of the Living One who sees me — rāʾâ held, and ḥay, “living,” standing where ʾēl stood.

16:15

wayyiqrāʾ ʾaḇrām šem-bᵉnô (“and Abram called the name of his son”): the angel told Hagar she would name him (verse 11), and it is Abram who does it. He uses the name he was given, so the instruction is carried out, but not by the person it was given to, and the chapter's last act returns the child to the father's household and Hagar to silence. She does not speak again until chapter 21, when she is sent away for good.
