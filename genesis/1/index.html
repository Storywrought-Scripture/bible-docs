Translation notes

Genesis 1

The Seven Days of Creation

1:1

bᵉrēʾšîṯ bārāʾ. The grammar allows two readings. Absolute: “In the beginning God created…” — an independent clause, reflected by the LXX, Vulgate, and the Masoretic disjunctive accent. Construct: “When God began to create the heavens and the earth — the earth being formless and empty…” — favoured because rēʾšîṯ is normally a construct noun and here lacks the article (so Rashi, NJPS, and the NRSV text — the absolute reading is what NRSV puts in its margin). The construct reading makes v. 1 a temporal clause, v. 2 a parenthesis, and v. 3 the main event; it describes God ordering a pre-existing chaos rather than asserting creation from nothing. I translated the absolute reading — it carries the weight of the ancient versions and reads as a deliberate, freestanding opening — but note that creatio ex nihilo is a doctrine this verse permits, not one its grammar forces. bārāʾ (qal perfect) is used in the OT only with God as subject: a verb reserved for divine creation.

1:2

tōhû wāḇōhû: a rhyming hendiadys — “formless and empty,” “welter and waste.” It denotes unproductive chaos and desolation, not moral evil. rûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm: rûaḥ = spirit / wind / breath, so either “the Spirit of God,” “a wind from God,” or (taking ʾĕlōhîm as a superlative) “a mighty wind.” The participle mᵉraḥep̄eṯ (“hovering, fluttering”) is used of an eagle over its young (Deut 32:11), which tips me toward the personal “hovered” over a meteorological “wind sweeping.” tᵉhôm (“the deep”) is linguistically related to Babylonian Tiamat but here demythologized — not a rival deity, simply the dark primeval waters.

1:5

yôm ʾeḥāḏ: literally “day one,” using the cardinal ʾeḥāḏ (“one”), not the ordinal riʾšôn (“first”). Days two through six switch to ordinals, so “day one” may mark this as the unique foundational day. Note too that days 1–5 are anarthrous (“a second day…”) while the sixth and seventh take the article (v. 31; 2:2), quietly flagging the climax of the sequence.

1:6

rāqîaʿ: from rāqaʿ, “to hammer / beat out,” as a smith beats metal into a thin sheet (Exod 39:3). The word pictures a solid, beaten-out vault holding back the upper waters — the sky-dome of ancient Near Eastern cosmology. “Firmament” (KJV, via Vulgate firmamentum) keeps the solidity; “expanse” (ESV/NIV) modernizes and softens it. I chose “dome” to render the cosmology the Hebrew assumes rather than retroject a modern atmosphere.

1:9

Manuscript variant. After “and it was so,” the LXX adds an execution formula: “and the water under the heaven was gathered into its gatherings, and the dry land appeared” — filling out the command with its fulfillment, a command-and-fulfillment rhythm the MT supplies elsewhere but omits here. The fragmentary 4QGenᵏ (4Q10) at this verse has been reconstructed (by spacing) as possibly carrying a longer text closer to the LXX, but it is too broken to be decisive. The terser MT is most likely original, with the LXX harmonizing to the chapter’s rhythm. I translated the MT.

1:14, 16

mᵉʾōrōṯ (“lights, luminaries”) — pointedly not the ordinary words for sun (šemeš) and moon (yārēaḥ), which double as the names of worshipped deities. The text calls them merely “the greater light” and “the lesser light,” lamps God hangs to “govern,” and tacks on the stars almost dismissively (“and the stars as well”). This is deliberate anti-astral polemic: the heavenly bodies are creations and timekeepers, not gods. môʿăḏîm (“appointed times”) is more than “seasons” — the same word names the sacred festival calendar (Lev 23), hence “sacred times.”

1:21

tannînim: “the great sea-monsters / sea-dragons.” In the surrounding mythologies the sea-dragon is the primeval chaos-foe the gods must defeat; here God simply makes them — one more item on the list, pronounced “good.” This is the second use of bārāʾ in the chapter (after v. 1), underlining that even the dreaded deeps and their monsters are his handiwork, not rival powers.

1:26–27

naʿăśeh (“let us make”): a first-person plural. The live options are (a) a plural of deliberation or majesty; (b) God addressing the divine council / heavenly court (cf. 1 Kgs 22:19; Job 1–2); (c) the later Christian reading of intra-Trinitarian speech. In its own setting the words would have conveyed (a) or (b); the text itself does not specify, so I kept “us / our” open rather than resolve it. ṣelem (“image”) is concrete — a statue, a representation; dᵉmûṯ (“likeness”) softens it to “resemblance.” In the ancient world a king set up his ṣelem in territory he ruled to mark his sovereign presence; humanity is God’s image placed in the world to represent his rule — which is why “image” runs straight into “let them rule.” In that setting the image language points naturally toward representative function, before it is about reason or soul. Note bārāʾ three times in v. 27 — the verb’s density marking the climax of creation.

1:26, 28

rāḏâ (“rule”) and kāḇaš (“subdue”) are both strong, even forceful verbs. rāḏâ is used of a king’s dominion; kāḇaš can mean to tread down, conquer, even subjugate (it is used of military subjugation elsewhere). I have not softened them — the mandate is real mastery over the earth. Whether that mandate implies stewardship or license is a theological question; the verbs themselves are vigorous.

1:27

“In his image… male and female”: the image is borne by humanity as male and female together. The verse does not gender the image; both sexes equally bear it.

1:29–30

The original provision is vegetarian: seed plants and fruit for humans, green plants for the animals. Meat is not granted until after the Flood (9:3). The text presents a peaceable initial order, worth flagging rather than reading past. kol ʾăšer bô nep̄eš ḥayyâ (v. 30, “everything that has the breath of life in it”) takes nep̄eš in its ordinary sense, against the glossary’s “being/creature”; recorded here as a departure. The standing flags at 9:4–5 and 12:5 do not reach this verse.

1:31

ṭôḇ mᵉʾōḏ (“very good”): the only day the verdict is intensified. Not “perfect” (a different concept) but “very good” — completely fit, fully answering its purpose.
