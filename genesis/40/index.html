Translation notes

Genesis 40

The Cupbearer and the Baker

40:1, 5

maš·qeh … ʾōp̄eh (“cupbearer” … “baker”): both are court offices of some rank — the cupbearer in particular is a position of trust, since he controls what the king drinks, and the same office appears in Nehemiah 1:11. The narrative is careful that these two men are placed in the house of the commander of the guard, which is Potiphar's own office (37:36; 39:1), and that Joseph is assigned to serve them. So the man who was thrown into this prison by that household is now handed the two royal prisoners whose fate will get him out of it, and the text arranges this without a word of comment on the irony.

40:8

hălôʾ lēʾlōhîm piṯrōnîm (“Do interpretations not belong to God?”): Joseph's one theological statement in the chapter, and it is a disclaimer. Dream interpretation in Egypt and Mesopotamia was a professional discipline with manuals and trained practitioners, and the two officials' complaint is precisely that there is no one to interpret — meaning no professional available in a prison. Joseph does not claim a technique; he claims that the meaning belongs to God and then asks to hear the dream. He says the same thing to Pharaoh at 41:16, more emphatically. The contrast the narrative is drawing is with the Egyptian experts, who at 41:8 will fail on both dreams.

40:13, 19, 20

yiśśāʾ p̄arʿōh ʾeṯ-rōʾšeḵā (“Pharaoh will lift up your head”): a single idiom used three times, and the second use turns it into something appalling. To “lift the head” of someone is to restore them to favour or to review and reinstate them; it may also carry the sense of a census, taking a count. Joseph uses it straight for the cupbearer at verse 13. For the baker he uses the same words and adds one, mēʿāleyḵā: yiśśāʾ p̄arʿōh ʾeṯ-rōʾšᵉḵā mēʿāleyḵā, “Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you.” Then verse 20 brings both men under a single wayyiśśāʾ — “he lifted up the head of the chief cupbearer and the head of the chief baker” — so that one verb describes a promotion and a decapitation in one clause. The pun is not decoration — it is how Joseph delivers a death sentence to a man who has just been encouraged by seeing the other's dream read favourably. English can carry it exactly, and does here.

40:15

kî-ḡunnōḇ gunnaḇtî (“For I was stolen away, stolen”): the emphatic passive, doubled. This is Joseph's own account of what happened to him, and he says he was stolen — not sold. That bears directly on the tangle at 37:28, where Midianite traders pass by, someone draws Joseph up out of the pit, and he is sold to Ishmaelites; on one reading of that verse the traders take him without the brothers' involvement. Joseph's word here is consistent with that reading, though he may simply be declining to accuse his own family before Egyptians. Note also habbôr, “the pit,” the same word used of the cistern his brothers threw him into at 37:24 — he uses it of the Egyptian prison, and the narrator lets the two holes share a name. Note too the phrase ʾereṣ hāʿiḇrîm, “the land of the Hebrews,” which occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible: ʿiḇrî is what outsiders call this family, as the note at 14:13 sets out, and here Joseph takes the word up himself, in front of Egyptians, and extends it to a territory no Hebrew governs. And note what he does not say: he claims innocence of any offence in Egypt and does not accuse Potiphar's wife.

40:16

sallê ḥōrî (“baskets of white bread”): the expression is uncertain. ḥōrî is generally taken from a root for whiteness, hence white or fine bread, which is the traditional rendering and the one I have followed; some connect it to a word for holes, giving wicker or openwork baskets. Note the detail that the baker speaks only after he sees the first interpretation come out well — wayyarʾ … kî ṭôḇ pāṯar. He volunteers his dream on the strength of his colleague's good news, which is what makes the answer he receives land as hard as it does.

40:19

wᵉṯālâ ʾôṯᵉḵā ʿal-ʿēṣ (“and hang you on a tree”): the verb covers hanging or impaling, and the sequence in the two verses — the head lifted off, then the body hung, then birds eating the flesh — describes execution followed by public exposure of the corpse. Denial of burial is a severe aggravation in this literature (compare Deuteronomy 21:22–23, which permits the exposure but requires the body down by nightfall). The birds are the detail that connects the interpretation to the dream: in the baker's dream birds were already eating out of the basket on his head, and Joseph reads them as themselves.

40:23

wᵉlōʾ-zāḵar śar-hammašqîm ʾeṯ-yôsēp̄ wayyiškāḥēhû (“And the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph. He forgot him.”): the same thing said twice, once negatively and once positively, which is the construction the book uses for emphasis at its most pointed moments — as at 11:30 of Sarai's barrenness. The verb is zāḵar, and the chapter turns on it: Joseph asks to be remembered at verse 14 (zᵉḵartanî, and again in the hip̄ʿîl, wᵉhizḵartanî, “mention me”), and verse 23 says the cupbearer did not. It is also the verb this book uses of God — of Noah at 8:1, of the covenant at 9:15 and 9:16, of Abraham at 19:29, of Rachel at 30:22, which is every occurrence in Genesis with God as its subject. In the three narrative instances the remembering is immediately acted on: a wind driven over the waters, Lot pulled out of the overthrow, Rachel's womb opened. It is acting on a commitment, not recovering a lost thought — see the note at 8:1. Joseph asked to be remembered in that sense, and the man who owed him everything did the opposite. The chapter ends there, and 41:1 opens with miqqēṣ šᵉnāṯayim yāmîm, “at the end of two full years.” The narrator gives no reassurance across that gap.
