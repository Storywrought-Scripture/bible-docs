Translation notes

Genesis 48

The Crossed Hands

48:5–6

ʾep̄rayim ûmᵉnaššeh kirʾûḇēn wᵉšimʿôn yihyû-lî (“Ephraim and Manasseh will be mine, as Reuben and Simeon are”): a formal adoption, and it is the mechanism by which Joseph receives the double portion of the firstborn without being called the firstborn. Two of his sons are promoted to the status of Jacob's own, so Joseph's line occupies two tribal allotments instead of one. Reuben, the actual firstborn, forfeited that share at 35:22 and is told why at 49:3–4; 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 states the transaction explicitly. Note the consequence for the tribal allotments: there is no single Joseph allotment — the land goes to Ephraim and Manasseh — and Levi holds no territory, which is how twelve remains twelve. The name itself survives in the lists: Numbers 13:11 has “the tribe of Joseph,” immediately glossed as Manasseh.

48:7

mēṯâ ʿālay rāḥēl (“Rachel died on me”): the sentence arrives from nowhere, in the middle of a legal adoption, and it is the key to the whole chapter. Jacob is explaining why Joseph's sons become his: Rachel died young with only two sons, and this is how her line is made two lines. ʿālay, “on me,” is the idiom of a loss falling on someone. He has been dead-set on Rachel's children since 37:3 and says so here with no embarrassment, at the moment of dividing an inheritance among twelve. On the geographical difficulty of the Ephrath–Bethlehem gloss, see the note at 35:19–20; it is repeated here word for word.

48:8–14

The scene is chapter 27 turned around, and the vocabulary makes it unmistakable. Jacob's eyes are kāḇᵉḏû mizzōqen, heavy with age so that he could not see — the same condition as Isaac's at 27:1, though the verb is different — there kāhâ, to grow dim, here kāḇēḏ, to be heavy. What the two share is the root z-q-n: Isaac is zāqēn, “old,” at 27:1, and Jacob's eyes are heavy mizzōqen, “from old age.” He asks mî-ʾēlleh, “who are these?”, as Isaac asked who are you, my son?. He kisses them and embraces them — where in the earlier scene it was the son who came close and kissed the blind father (27:26–27), and there was no embrace at all. And then the man who once exploited a blind father's inability to tell two sons apart deliberately crosses his own hands to put the younger ahead of the elder — with his eyes shut and knowing exactly what he is doing. śikkēl ʾeṯ-yāḏāyw is “he crossed his hands,” from a verb suggesting acting shrewdly or with understanding. Where the earlier scene was fraud, this one is open, deliberate, and done over the objection of a son who can see perfectly well.

48:15–16

hammalʾāḵ haggōʾēl ʾōṯî mikkol-rāʿ (“the messenger who has redeemed me from all harm”): the blessing is built as three parallel lines naming God three ways — the God his fathers walked before, the God who has been his shepherd, and the messenger who redeemed him — and the third then takes a singular verb, yᵉḇārēḵ, “may he bless.” The relation between the messenger and God is left as unresolved here as it is at 16:7–13 and 22:11–15, and — with “a man” standing in place of the messenger — at 32:24–30. Two firsts are worth marking. This is the first time God is called a shepherd in the Bible, by a man who spent twenty years as one. And it is the first occurrence of gāʾal, to redeem — the verb of the kinsman-redeemer in Ruth, of the exodus in Exodus 6:6, and of the great redemption passages in Isaiah. Its root sense is a relative buying back what belongs to the family, and Jacob applies it to his own life. One image in the blessing is not carried in the English: wᵉyiḏgû lārōḇ, rendered “let them grow into a multitude,” is from dāḡâ, a verb formed from dāḡ, fish — “let them teem.”

48:14, 19

wᵉʾûlām ʾāḥîw haqqāṭōn yiḡdal mimmennû (“But his younger brother will be greater than he”): the chapter uses two words for the younger — haṣṣāʿîr at verse 14, haqqāṭōn here. This is one of the book's reversals of primogeniture — Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Perez over Zerah, Joseph over ten elder brothers, Ephraim over Manasseh here, and Reuben stripped and Judah raised in the oracles of 49:3–4 and 49:8–10. Jacob's yāḏaʿtî ḇᵉnî yāḏaʿtî, “I know, my son, I know,” is the clearest statement in Genesis that a reversal of the birth order is intended rather than accidental or contested. The history bears it out: Ephraim becomes the dominant northern tribe, and the prophets use “Ephraim” as a name for the whole northern kingdom. Note that Manasseh is not disinherited — he too will become a people, and he too will be great. Genesis never simply discards the elder line.

48:20

bᵉḵā yᵉḇārēḵ yiśrāʾēl (“By you Israel will pronounce blessing”): the narrator records the origin of a formula still in use — the blessing spoken over sons in Jewish practice to this day. It is one of the few places in Genesis where a saying is explicitly said to have entered common speech, alongside “like Nimrod, a mighty hunter” (10:9) and “on the mountain of the Lord it will be seen” (22:14).

48:22

šᵉḵem ʾaḥaḏ ʿal-ʾaḥeyḵā (“one shoulder of land more than your brothers”): a deliberate pun, and it cannot be carried in English. šᵉḵem means shoulder, and by extension a ridge or a portion of land; it is also Shechem, the city — which is where Joseph's bones are in fact buried (Joshua 24:32), on the plot Jacob bought at 33:19. So the phrase means both “one portion more” and “Shechem.” The second half is harder: which I took from the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow. Genesis records no such conquest by Jacob — chapter 33 has him buying that land, and chapter 34 has his sons take the city by treachery, in an act he did not condemn at the time — his one word at 34:30 was about the danger to himself — and condemns only on his deathbed at 49:5–7. Proposals: a lost tradition, a reference to the Shechem episode claimed as his own, or a prophetic perfect describing the later conquest. The tension is left standing rather than resolved.
