Translation notes

Genesis 29

Leah and Rachel

29:2–12

The scene is a type-scene: a traveller arrives in a foreign land, meets a young woman at a well, water is drawn, she runs home with the news, and a betrothal follows. Genesis has already run it once for Isaac through the servant (chapter 24), and Exodus 2:15–21 runs it again for Moses at the well in Midian. Recognising the pattern is what makes the variations legible, and here they are pointed. In chapter 24 the servant prayed and the sign was granted; here nobody prays and God is not mentioned. There the woman watered ten camels for a stranger; here the man does the drawing. And the stone that normally takes several shepherds to move (verses 3 and 8) is rolled away by Jacob alone at the sight of Rachel — the scene's only comment on the strength of his feeling, made by mechanics rather than by adjective. Note the threefold Laban his mother's brother in verse 10, which reads as deliberate insistence on the kinship.

29:11

wayyiššaq yaʿăqōḇ lᵉrāḥēl wayyiśśāʾ ʾeṯ-qōlô wayyēḇk (“And Jacob kissed Rachel, and he raised his voice and wept”): he kisses her before he tells her who he is, and the weeping is unexplained. Readings differ — relief at journey's end, recognition that God has kept the promise of 28:15, grief at arriving with nothing where his grandfather's servant once arrived with ten camels of gifts. The narrator supplies none of them. Note also that Jacob arrives empty-handed, which becomes important when Laban starts naming a price.

29:17

wᵉʿênê lēʾâ rakkôṯ (“And Leah's eyes were soft”): genuinely uncertain, and the choice shapes how a reader sees her for the rest of the book. rak means soft, tender, delicate. The standard English versions differ, several rendering it negatively — ESV and NIV have “weak” — and reading the verse as a contrast in which Leah is plain and Rachel beautiful. But rak nowhere denotes a physical blemish; of a person it means delicate or tender, and of the heart, faint (Deuteronomy 20:8). And the sentence simply places the two sisters side by side. A serious reading takes it as praise — soft or lovely eyes, her one notable beauty, set against Rachel who was beautiful all over. The Septuagint reads astheneis, weak. I have rendered it “soft,” which keeps the Hebrew's own ambiguity, and record that the traditional reading is an interpretive decision rather than a lexical fact.

29:23–26

wᵉhinnēh-hîʾ lēʾâ (“And in the morning — there was Leah”): the disclosure is three words in Hebrew and it lands like a blow, with wᵉhinnēh putting the reader inside Jacob's eyes at the moment of discovery. The whole episode is chapter 27 turned around on him, and Laban's explanation makes the point unmistakable without the narrator having to: lōʾ-yēʿāśeh ḵēn bimqômēnû lāṯēṯ haṣṣᵉʿîrâ lip̄nê habbᵉḵîrâ — “It is not done so in our place, to give the younger before the firstborn.” Jacob obtained the blessing of the firstborn by presenting a younger son as an elder in the dark, exploiting a father who could not see; he is now given an elder daughter in place of a younger, in the dark, and discovers it at daybreak. His protest — lāmmâ rimmîṯānî, “why have you deceived me?” — uses the cognate verb of the noun Isaac used of him at 27:35, mirmâ. The narrator makes no comment at all; the vocabulary does the work.

29:27

mallēʾ šᵉḇuaʿ zōʾṯ (“Finish this one's week”): the bridal week, the seven days of wedding celebration, so Jacob receives Rachel a week later and then works the second seven years afterward rather than beforehand. The chapter is saturated with sevens — seven years, a week, seven years again — which is the same numerical patterning that runs through the flood narrative. Note that the marriage to two sisters at once is later prohibited outright by Leviticus 18:18, which forbids taking a woman as a rival to her sister during her lifetime. As with the standing stone at 28:18, Genesis records the patriarchs doing what the law will forbid and does not comment.

29:31

śᵉnûʾâ (“unloved”): the passive participle of śānēʾ, “to hate,” and the translation problem is that the word functions here as a legal and relative term, not as a description of feeling. Deuteronomy 21:15–17 legislates for a man with two wives, “one loved and one unloved,” and protects the inheritance of the firstborn son of the unloved one — which is precisely this household's situation, and the very right Jacob himself took from Esau. Verse 30 has just said Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah, so “hated” in the modern sense overstates it. I have rendered it “unloved,” which carries the comparative sense the passage requires. The verse also states the pattern the book keeps: God attends to the one who is passed over.

29:32–35

The four names are given etymologies by Leah, and each is a sound-play rather than a derivation. Reuben — rᵉʾûḇēn, heard as rāʾâ, “he has seen,” with a second echo of bᵉʿonyî, “my affliction,” and the plain reading rᵉʾû ḇēn, “see, a son.” Simeon — šimʿôn from šāmaʿ, “heard.” Levi — lēwî from lāwâ, “to be joined.” Judah — yᵉhûḏâ from yāḏâ, “to praise.” What the sequence records is a woman's four attempts to be loved, and the narrator lets the explanations speak: the first two names are about being seen and heard by God in her affliction, the third is still about her husband — now this time my husband will be joined to me — and the fourth stops asking for anything and simply praises. She is not said to be loved at any point. The tribe that carries the monarchy and the tribe that carries the priesthood both come from the wife who was not chosen.
