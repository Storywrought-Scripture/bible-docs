Translation notes

Genesis 34

Dinah, and the Sword of Simeon and Levi

34:1

wattēṣēʾ dînâ … lirʾôṯ biḇnôṯ hāʾāreṣ (“And Dinah … went out to see the daughters of the land”): she is identified by her mother rather than her father, which is unusual, and the verse gives her one action and one purpose. A long interpretive tradition has treated the going out as itself culpable; nothing in the Hebrew supports that, and the narrator supplies no criticism. It should be said plainly at the outset that Dinah does not speak anywhere in this chapter, is never asked anything, and is last named in verse 26, when she is taken out of a house. She appears twice more and never again by name, both times as the object of a verb — “their sister” in verse 27, “our sister” in the chapter's last line. Everything that happens is negotiated about her by men.

34:2

wayyiqqaḥ ʾōṯāh wayyiškaḇ ʾōṯāh wayᵉʿannehā (“he took her and lay with her and violated her”): three verbs, and the third is the one that carries the weight. ʿinnâ in the piel means to afflict, humiliate, or force down; it is the verb used of Israel's oppression in Egypt at Exodus 1:11–12, and in sexual contexts at Deuteronomy 22:29 and 2 Samuel 13:12–14, where Amnon's use of force is explicit. Deuteronomy 22:24 uses it as well, of a case the law itself treats as consensual — the girl is condemned there “because she did not cry out in the town” — so what the verb marks is a sexual wrong done to a woman, not force as such. Note also that šāḵaḇ is here construed with the direct object marker ʾōṯāh rather than with the preposition ʿim, “with,” and some read that construction as marking a forced act. It will not carry the argument alone: it is used of ordinary intercourse at Leviticus 15:18 and of adultery at Numbers 5:13. The weight falls on ʿinnâ. A minority reading argues that the sequence describes an elopement or a seduction that dishonoured the family rather than a rape, resting on the tenderness of verse 3, on the fact that ʿinnâ can describe degradation of status, and on the absence from this verse of the seizure verbs the law uses where it does mark force — ḥāzaq at Deuteronomy 22:25 and tāp̄aś at 22:28, both rendered “seizes” in this volume. I have translated ʿinnâ as “violated,” which is what the verb's use of a woman elsewhere supports, and record the dissent rather than concealing it. The project's rule against sanitising applies here as much as anywhere.

34:3

wattiḏbaq nap̄šô … wayᵉḏabbēr ʿal-lēḇ hannaʿărâ (“And he clung to Dinah … and he spoke to the girl's heart”): the verse immediately after the assault attributes to Shechem clinging, love, and tender speech — dāḇaq is the verb of 2:24, a man clinging to his wife, and “to speak to the heart” is to speak reassuringly or wooingly. The juxtaposition is deliberately unbearable and the narrator makes no attempt to reconcile it. It is worth resisting the two easy moves: taking verse 3 as evidence that verse 2 was not an assault, or dismissing verse 3 as insincere. The text asserts both, and what it thereby describes — a man who takes a woman by force and then wants to keep her — is neither rare nor incoherent. Contrast 2 Samuel 13:15, where Amnon's love for Tamar turns to hatred immediately afterward; the Hebrew Bible knows both patterns and reports each without comment.

34:5, 30

wᵉheḥĕrîš yaʿăqōḇ (“and Jacob kept silent”): Jacob's silence is stated and never explained, and when he does finally speak, at verse 30, every word of it is about consequences to himself — his reputation, his safety, his numbers. He does not mention Dinah, and he does not mention right or wrong. The narrator lets that stand as the whole of his response. His deathbed word at 49:5–7 does eventually condemn Simeon and Levi, and it condemns them for violence and anger, and still says nothing of their sister.

34:7

nᵉḇālâ bᵉyiśrāʾēl (“an outrage in Israel”): nᵉḇālâ is not simple folly but disgraceful, order-breaking conduct — it is the term used of the crime at Gibeah in Judges 20:6 and by Tamar in 2 Samuel 13:12. The phrase “in Israel” is an anachronism: Israel is at this point one man with twelve children, not a people with a standard of conduct, and the expression belongs to the settled national period. It is one of the clearer places where the narrator's own era shows through, comparable to the Deuteronomic vocabulary at 26:5. wᵉḵēn lōʾ yēʿāśeh, “a thing that is not done,” is the language of a community's settled boundaries.

34:13

bᵉmirmâ (“with deceit”): the same noun Isaac used of Jacob at 27:35 — bāʾ ʾāḥîḵā bᵉmirmâ, “your brother came in deceit.” The narrator applies his father's word to the sons, and the method is his method: a religious sign used as an instrument of a trick. The chapter is careful to say that the deceit was because he had defiled Dinah their sister, which states the motive without approving the means. Note that circumcision — the covenant sign given in chapter 17 — is here turned into a weapon, and the narrator neither defends nor denounces that.

34:23

miqnēhem wᵉqinyānām … hălôʾ lānû hēm (“Their livestock and their property … will they not be ours?”): Hamor and Shechem sell the proposal to their townsmen with an argument they did not make to Jacob's family — that absorbing these newcomers will eventually absorb their wealth. So the men of the city agree to be circumcised out of calculation, and are then destroyed by men who proposed it out of calculation. The narrator gives both sides a concealed motive and lets the reader see both. Note that ûsᵉḥārûhā in Hamor's first offer at verse 10, rendered “move about in it,” is from a root meaning to go about or travel around, and from there to trade; the standard English versions read “trade in it.” I have kept the movement sense, which the parallel with “settle” and “take holdings” supports — but on the trading reading the commercial motive is already in the offer he makes to Jacob's family, and not only in the one he makes to his own townsmen.

34:25–29

wayyaharḡû kol-zāḵār (“and killed every male”): Simeon and Levi kill the men; then “Jacob's sons” — the wider group, entering after the killing — plunder the city, take the flocks and the wealth, and carry off the women and children as captives. The narrative distinguishes the two acts and reports the second in the same flat tone as the first. The reprisal is grotesquely disproportionate by the text's own arithmetic: one man's crime, an entire town's men dead, and their wives and children taken by the brothers of a woman who was herself taken. The chapter does not say this; it lays the sequence out and stops. Two details in the Hebrew are worth having. beṭaḥ at verse 25, rendered “unopposed,” is the word for security — it is ambiguous whether it describes the brothers coming in safety or the city lying secure and unsuspecting, and the standard versions divide between the two. And verse 27 shifts the verb to the plural, ṭimmᵉʾû, “they had defiled,” against the singular of verses 5 and 13: the narrator extends the offence from Shechem to the town. That is as close as the chapter comes to supplying a rationale, and it stops there.

34:31

haḵᵉzônâ yaʿăśeh ʾeṯ-ʾăḥôṯēnû (“Should he treat our sister like a whore?”): the last line of the chapter, and it is a question that nobody answers. Jacob does not reply; the narrative simply moves to 35:1. This is one of the few places in Genesis where a moral argument is left standing unrebutted at the end of a scene, and the effect is that the reader is handed the judgement. Set beside it the fact that the brothers are the only characters in the chapter who name a wrong done to her at all — and the fact that they made it with swords, over a town that had accepted their terms, and that fourteen verses earlier they had called her bittēnû, “our daughter,” a thing to be taken back. Genesis 49:7 gives Jacob's eventual verdict on them: “Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their fury, for it is cruel.” It does not give a verdict on the question they asked. Note too what is absent from the whole chapter: God is not mentioned once in its thirty-one verses, by the narrator or by anyone in it — in a chapter about an assault, a covenant sign turned into a trap, and a massacre. 35:1 opens with God speaking to Jacob again.
