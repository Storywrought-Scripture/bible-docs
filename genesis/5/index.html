Translation notes

Genesis 5

From Adam to Noah — and Enoch, Who Walked with God

5:1–2

sēp̄er tôlᵉḏōṯ (“the record of the family line”): tôlᵉḏōṯ here heads a written genealogy (cf. 2:4). The passage deliberately echoes 1:26–28: God makes humankind biḏmûṯ ʾĕlōhîm (“in the likeness of God”), creates them male and female, and blesses them. Notably God names the species ʾāḏām (“Humankind”) — the same word that becomes the man’s name. Of the 1:26 pair (image and likeness), only dᵉmûṯ, the softer term, is used here.

5:3

biḏmûṯô kᵉṣalmô (“in his own likeness, after his image”): the exact pair from 1:26 is now applied to Adam fathering Seth. The parallel is deliberate — God makes Adam in God’s likeness (5:1), Adam fathers Seth in Adam’s likeness (5:3) — declaring that the image of God is transmitted through human generation even after the fall; what passes down is at once the divine image and Adam’s now-fallen likeness.

The lifespans (5:3–32) These ages are the most-discussed text-critical feature of the chapter, because the three main witnesses disagree systematically. I translate the Masoretic figures. The Septuagint generally raises the “begetting age” of the early patriarchs by 100 years (Adam fathers Seth at 230, not 130, and so on through Mahalalel and Enoch), usually trimming the remaining years to keep the totals close — yielding a far longer span from creation to the Flood (about 2,240 years in the LXX against about 1,656 in the MT). The Samaritan Pentateuch follows a third scheme, with lower totals (about 1,307 years) and figures arranged so that none of the patriarchs outlives the Flood. The very regularity of the differences (round 100-year shifts) shows the variation is schematic — deliberate adjustment within one or more traditions, not random scribal error — and which scheme is original is genuinely debated. One striking MT datum: Methuselah’s 969 years, reckoned from his listed dates, run out in the very year of the Flood. Read all these totals as carrying that uncertainty.

5:22, 24

wayyiṯhallēḵ … ʾeṯ-hāʾĕlōhîm (“walked with God”): the hithpael of hālaḵ, the same form used of God “moving about” in the garden (3:8) and later of Noah (6:9). To “walk with God” is sustained, intimate fellowship; in Genesis only Enoch and Noah are said to do it. The refrain “and he died” that closes every other entry is conspicuously broken here: wᵉʾênennû kî-lāqaḥ ʾōṯô ʾĕlōhîm — “and he was no more, because God took him.” The Hebrew is reticent: it does not say he died but that God lāqaḥ (“took”) him. The LXX renders it metethēken (“transferred, translated”) — the root of the later language of Enoch’s “translation” without death (Hebrews 11:5). His 365 years also catch the eye, matching the days of the solar year — fitting for the figure later tradition tied to calendar lore.

5:29

Nōaḥ (“Noah”) is linked by Lamech to nāḥam (“to comfort, bring relief”): “this one will bring us relief” (yᵉnaḥămēnû). Strictly the name relates to nûaḥ (“rest”), so the play is loose — comfort and rest in the same orbit. The naming reaches back to 3:17: relief is sought “from the painful toil (ʿiṣṣāḇôn) of our hands, out of the ʾăḏāmâ that the Lord has cursed” — the same word for toil and the same cursed ground. In a chapter whose drumbeat is “and he died,” Lamech’s hope names a coming relief; Noah will indeed mark a new beginning (and, pointedly, the first vineyard, 9:20).
