Translation notes

Genesis 4

Cain and Abel; the Line of Cain; the Birth of Seth

4:1

Three things. (1) yāḏaʿ (“knew”) is the Hebrew euphemism for sexual union — earthy and direct. (2) Qayin (“Cain”) plays on qānîṯî (“I have gotten, acquired, produced”): she names him for the verb of getting. (3) ʾeṯ YHWH is genuinely hard: ʾeṯ can be the preposition “with” (“a man with the Lord’s help,” so most translations, the Targums, and LXX dia tou theou, “through God”), or the direct-object marker, yielding the startling “I have gotten a man — the Lord” (as if she thinks she has borne a divine deliverer). I rendered the consensus “with the Lord’s help,” but the bare ʾeṯ leaves the bolder reading open.

4:2

Heḇel (“Abel”) is the word for “vapour, breath, fleeting nothing” — the heḇel of Ecclesiastes. Unlike Cain’s, his name is given no explanation; the name itself is the omen — a life as brief as a breath. Note the pairing: Cain works the ʾăḏāmâ (the ground just cursed in 3:17), Abel keeps flocks.

4:3–5

The text quietly distinguishes the two offerings. Abel brings “the firstborn of his flock and their fat portions” — the choice, the best; Cain brings simply “from the fruit of the ground,” with no note of first or best. The narrative never states outright why God regarded one and not the other, but the descriptions lean that way (Hebrews 11:4 locates it in faith). šāʿâ (“looked at, gazed”) is “regarded with favour”; minḥâ is a “gift, tribute,” not yet the technical grain offering of Leviticus 2.

4:7

One of the hardest verses in the chapter — and the hinge with 3:16. śᵉʾēṯ (“a lifting up,” from nāśāʾ) sets “lifted up” against Cain’s “fallen face” (4:5–6) — acceptance, or the lifting of guilt. ḥaṭṭāʾṯ rōḇēṣ (“sin crouching”) personifies sin as a predator at the door; rōḇēṣ (“crouching”) is masculine though ḥaṭṭāʾṯ (“sin”) is feminine, and the word is cognate with Akkadian rābiṣu, a demon that lurks at thresholds — “sin is a crouching demon at the door.” Decisively, “its desire (tᵉšûqāṯô) is for you, but you must rule (timšāl) over it” is the very pair from 3:16 — tᵉšûqâ + māšal — now unmistakably a struggle for mastery: sin longs to dominate Cain, and he is charged to dominate it. That this same vocabulary describes sin’s predatory drive is the strongest internal argument for reading the woman’s “desire” in 3:16 as a bid for control. (The LXX rendered the whole verse differently, about rightly dividing the sacrifice.)

4:8

Manuscript variant, the classic one. MT reads only “And Cain said to his brother Abel” — then jumps to “when they were in the field,” with no words spoken; the sentence has a hole where Cain’s speech should be. The Samaritan Pentateuch, LXX, Peshitta, Vulgate, and Targums all preserve “Let us go out to the field.” Almost certainly the MT lost the clause (a scribe’s eye slipping), and I supplied it from the versions. The alternative is to leave the MT’s abrupt gap, which some keep to preserve the received text.

4:9

šōmēr (“keeper”): Cain’s retort “Am I my brother’s keeper?” uses šāmar — the same charge (“to keep, guard”) given the human in 2:15 and assigned to the cherubim in 3:24. He denies the very vocation woven into the human calling.

4:10

dᵉmê (“bloods,” plural): the Hebrew is plural — “the bloods of your brother” — an intensive plural for violently shed blood (later tradition heard in it the blood of Abel’s unborn descendants too). The plural verb “are crying out” personifies the spilled blood as a voice demanding justice from the ʾăḏāmâ that drank it.

4:11–12

ʾārûr ʾattâ (“cursed are you”): the curse-word now lands on a human being for the first time — in 3:14, 17 it fell on the serpent and the ground; here Cain himself is cursed “from the ground.” nāʿ wānāḏ (“a restless wanderer,” KJV “fugitive and vagabond”) is a rhyming pair from two roots, nûaʿ (“to totter”) and nûḏ (“to wander, totter”) — and it sets up the land of “Nod” (Wandering) in 4:16.

4:13–14

gāḏôl ʿăwōnî minnᵉśōʾ: ʿāwōn is “iniquity, guilt, punishment for guilt,” and nᵉśōʾ (from nāśāʾ, “to lift, bear”) makes the line either “my punishment is too great to bear” or “my guilt is too great to be forgiven” — both senses live in the one root. It is also a grim echo of 4:7’s śᵉʾēṯ: the “lifting up” Cain spurned has become a weight too heavy to be lifted. gēraštā (“you have driven me out”) is the gāraš of 3:24 — Cain expelled from the ground as his parents were from the garden.

4:15

“Not so”: MT reads lāḵēn (“therefore”), but the LXX and Samaritan Pentateuch read lōʾ ḵēn (“not so!”) — God countering Cain’s fear of death with a flat refusal. I followed the versional “Not so,” which fits the rhetorical turn; MT’s “therefore” yields the same protective outcome. The ʾôṯ (“sign, mark”) on Cain is, in origin, protective — a guard against vengeance, not a brand of shame; the text never says what it was.

4:16

Nôḏ (“Wandering”): the “land of Nod” is, by its very name, “the land of Wandering” — the restless wanderer (nāʿ wānāḏ, 4:12) comes to dwell in Wander-land. “East of Eden” continues the eastward drift away from God’s presence (3:24).

4:17–22

Cain’s line is the cradle of civilisation: a city (4:17), tent-herding (Jabal), music (Jubal, lyre and pipe), and metallurgy (Tubal-Cain, bronze and iron). Culture’s gifts arise from the cursed and exiled line — the text reports this without praise or blame, an honest ambivalence worth teaching. Note Lamech (4:19) as the first polygamist, taking two wives.

4:23–24

Lamech’s “Song of the Sword” is the first human poem after Adam’s (2:23), and its content is vengeance. The parallelism (a man // a young man; wounding // bruising) shows the disproportion: he kills for a mere injury and boasts of it to his wives. Where Cain’s sevenfold vengeance was God’s protection, Lamech seizes “seventy-sevenfold” for himself — violence escalating across the generations. (Jesus deliberately inverts the figure in Matthew 18:22: forgive “seventy-seven times.”)

4:25–26

Šēṯ (“Seth”) plays on šāṯ (“he set, appointed, placed”): “God has appointed (šāṯ) another seed” — and zeraʿ (“seed”) deliberately recalls the contested seed of 3:15; the line of promise continues past Abel’s death. ʾĕnôš means “man, mortal” — humanity in its frailty. The chapter closes: “people began to call on the name of YHWH” — hûḥal (from ḥālal, “to begin”; a minority reads the same root as “to profane”). This is the first communal worship invoking the Lord by name — sitting in tension with Exodus 6:3, where the name is said to be newly revealed to Moses (a classic source-critical datum).
